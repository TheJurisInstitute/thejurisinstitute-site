Legal Research Education

What Is Legal Research and Why Does It Matter?

Legal research is the process of finding and analyzing the legal authorities — statutes, case law, regulations, and secondary sources — that govern a particular legal issue. Every legal argument, every court filing, and every legal document rests on a foundation of legal authority. An argument without legal support is opinion; an argument backed by controlling legal authority is advocacy.

Legal research involves two distinct challenges: finding the law that applies to your specific issue, and understanding how courts have interpreted and applied that law. A statute may appear to clearly answer a question, only to have been interpreted in an unexpected way by the courts. A court opinion that seems on-point may have been reversed on appeal or distinguished so many times that it no longer represents good law. Professional legal research locates the controlling authorities and verifies that they remain good law.

The output of legal research is typically a legal research memorandum — a structured written document that presents the issue, summarizes the applicable law, applies that law to the specific facts, and reaches a conclusion. Legal memoranda are used to brief lawyers on an unfamiliar area of law, to support motions and briefs, to educate clients on their legal rights, and to analyze legal risk before taking action.

The Juris Institute provides legal research services for individuals, pro se litigants, attorneys seeking research support, and anyone who needs to understand what the law says on a specific issue. We do not provide legal advice — our research identifies what the law says, not what you should do about it. That distinction is fundamental to what we offer.

Primary vs. Secondary Legal Sources

Primary legal sources are the law itself — constitutions, statutes, regulations, and court opinions. Secondary sources are materials that explain and analyze the law — law review articles, legal encyclopedias, treatises, and practice guides. Effective legal research begins with secondary sources to understand the legal landscape, then moves to primary sources to find the controlling authority. Our research identifies and cites both, giving you a complete picture of the legal landscape on your issue.

Jurisdiction Matters

Legal research is always jurisdiction-specific. The law governing your issue depends on which court would hear your case — federal or state, and if state, which state. A legal principle that is well-established in one jurisdiction may be an open question in another. We research the specific jurisdiction you identify and clearly distinguish between controlling authority (courts in your jurisdiction whose decisions must be followed) and persuasive authority (courts in other jurisdictions whose reasoning may be cited but is not binding).

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between legal research and legal advice?

Legal research identifies and explains what the law says on a specific issue — the statutes, cases, and regulations that apply. Legal advice applies the law to your specific facts and circumstances and recommends a course of action. For example, legal research might identify that Georgia courts require fraud claims to be pled with particularity and explain what that standard requires. Legal advice would tell you whether your specific facts are sufficient to survive a motion to dismiss. We provide the research; applying it to your specific situation and deciding what to do about it is the advice that requires a licensed attorney.

How long does legal research take?

The time required for legal research depends on the complexity of the issue, the jurisdiction, and the depth of analysis requested. A targeted research question in a well-developed area of law — what are the elements of a breach of contract claim in Georgia? — may be completed in a few hours. A multi-issue research project covering an unsettled area of law or multiple jurisdictions may take several days. We provide a timeline estimate with every project quote.

What format do you deliver research in?

Legal research is delivered as a written research memorandum in the standard IRAC format — Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion. The memo identifies the issue you asked about, states the applicable rule of law with full citations to primary authority, applies the rule to the facts you provided, and reaches a conclusion. For simpler research requests, we deliver an annotated case list or statute summary with explanatory notes. We discuss the best format for your specific project at the time of the quote.

Can you research issues in any state?

Yes. We conduct legal research for all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and federal law. Every research project is jurisdiction-specific — we identify which jurisdiction's law applies to your issue and research the controlling authority in that jurisdiction. For issues that span multiple jurisdictions or that involve both federal and state law, we address each separately and explain how the different bodies of law interact.

Can you verify whether a case is still good law?

Yes. Case law verification — confirming that a case has not been overruled, reversed, or so heavily criticized that it no longer represents good law — is part of every case law research project we deliver. We use citator services (the equivalent of Shepard's or KeyCite) to verify the current status of every case we cite. Citing overruled precedent is one of the most damaging errors in a legal brief; we verify each citation before delivering the research.
