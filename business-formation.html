Business Formation Education

Why Business Formation Documents Matter

Starting a business requires more than filing the right forms with the state. The documents that govern your business — the operating agreement for an LLC, the bylaws and shareholder agreement for a corporation — determine how decisions get made, how profits are distributed, what happens when owners disagree, and how the business can be sold, transferred, or dissolved. These are not formalities. They are the foundational rules of your business.

The most common and costly mistakes in business formation are not choosing the wrong entity type — they are failing to have the governing documents at all, or having documents that were copied from a template and never tailored to the actual business. An LLC operating agreement that does not address buy-sell provisions leaves co-owners with no agreed mechanism if one partner wants to exit. Bylaws that do not address quorum requirements for shareholder meetings can paralyze corporate decision-making when owners disagree.

Business formation documents serve another critical function: establishing the separation between the business entity and its owners that provides personal liability protection. Courts will look past the corporate or LLC structure and hold owners personally liable — a doctrine called 'piercing the corporate veil' — when the entity was not properly formed, when proper records were not maintained, or when the entity and its owners comingled assets and finances.

The Juris Institute prepares business formation documents based on your business structure, ownership arrangement, and operating instructions. We do not advise you on which entity type is right for your business or your tax situation — that requires an accountant and attorney familiar with your specific circumstances. What we do is prepare the governing documents that establish your chosen entity's structure and rules.

LLC vs. Corporation: Document Implications

LLCs are governed by an operating agreement, which is a flexible document that can be structured in almost any way the members agree — member-managed or manager-managed, single-member or multi-member, profit distributions on any schedule and in any proportion. Corporations are governed by bylaws (which establish procedural rules) and a shareholder agreement (which addresses ownership rights and restrictions). Corporations also require minutes of meetings, stock issuance records, and other corporate formalities. Both entity types require proper documentation to maintain liability protection.

Single-Member vs. Multi-Member Operating Agreements

Even a single-member LLC should have an operating agreement. Without one, the default rules of your state's LLC statute apply — which may not reflect your intentions and may not provide adequate structure for banking, financing, or eventual sale of the business. For multi-member LLCs, a properly drafted operating agreement is even more critical: it must address how new members can join, how existing members can exit, what happens if a member dies or divorces, how disputes are resolved, and how the business can be wound up.

Who We Serve

Who Needs Business Formation Documents?

Every business owner who has chosen an entity structure — LLC, corporation, or partnership — needs governing documents that establish how the business operates and protect personal liability.

New Business Owners

Entrepreneurs forming a new LLC or corporation need governing documents before the business begins operating. Without an operating agreement or bylaws, your business operates on your state's default rules — which may not reflect your intentions.

Co-Founders and Business Partners

When two or more people start a business together, a written governing agreement is the single most important document they can have. Co-founder disputes are among the most common causes of early business failure. A properly drafted operating or shareholder agreement addresses the difficult questions before they become disputes.

Existing Businesses Without Documents

Many businesses operate without proper governing documents — either because they were never prepared, because template documents were filed and then forgotten, or because the business has grown beyond what its original documents anticipated. We prepare updated, comprehensive documents for businesses at any stage.

Sole Proprietors Converting to an LLC

Sole proprietors who convert to an LLC gain personal liability protection — but only if the LLC is properly documented and operated as a separate entity. We prepare operating agreements for newly formed single-member LLCs and the documents needed to transfer existing business assets into the entity.

Investors and Minority Shareholders

Minority shareholders and investors in closely held businesses need shareholder agreements that protect their investment — addressing what happens to their shares if the company is sold, their right to participate in future fundraising, and their ability to sell their shares.

Businesses Adding New Owners

When an existing LLC or corporation brings in new members or shareholders, the governing documents must be updated. We prepare membership interest assignment documents, operating agreement amendments, and updated shareholder agreements that reflect the new ownership structure.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need an operating agreement even for a single-member LLC?

Yes. Even if you are the only member of your LLC, an operating agreement is important. It establishes the LLC's structure, confirms that the entity is separate from you personally, and is often required by banks when you open a business bank account or apply for financing. Without an operating agreement, your state's default LLC rules govern your business — and those rules are designed for the general case, not your specific situation. Most banks require an operating agreement before they will open an account for an LLC.

What is the difference between bylaws and a shareholder agreement?

Bylaws are the procedural rules of a corporation — they govern how meetings are held, how votes are taken, how officers are appointed, and how the board of directors operates. They are generally public documents filed with or available through the state. A shareholder agreement governs the relationship between shareholders — it is a private contract among the owners that addresses stock transfer restrictions, buy-sell rights, and other ownership matters. A corporation typically needs both. Bylaws without a shareholder agreement leave critical ownership issues unaddressed; a shareholder agreement without bylaws leaves corporate governance undefined.

What is an S-Corporation election and do I need it?

An S-Corporation is not a separate type of business entity — it is a tax election that a qualifying corporation (or LLC) makes with the IRS. S-Corp status allows the business's income and losses to pass through to shareholders for tax purposes, rather than being taxed at the corporate level. S-Corps can also allow owner-employees to pay themselves a reasonable salary and take additional profits as distributions, potentially reducing self-employment tax. S-Corp status has eligibility requirements and restrictions, and the tax benefits depend on your business's specific income profile. We prepare the underlying corporate documents and the IRS Form 2553 for the S-Corp election; whether the election makes sense for your business is a question for your accountant.

What should a buy-sell agreement cover?

A buy-sell agreement (also called a buyout agreement) establishes the mechanism for one owner to purchase another's interest in the business — triggered by events such as death, disability, divorce, voluntary departure, or retirement. The key provisions are: what events trigger the buyout, how the purchase price is determined (fixed price, formula, or appraisal), how the purchase is financed (installment payments, life insurance, etc.), and the timeline for the transaction. A buy-sell agreement without a clear valuation mechanism is often worse than none, because it substitutes the valuation dispute for the underlying ownership dispute.

How do I maintain my LLC's liability protection?

To maintain the personal liability protection an LLC provides, you must: (1) keep the LLC's finances separate from your personal finances — maintain a dedicated business bank account and never commingle funds; (2) properly document major business decisions with written resolutions or meeting minutes; (3) enter into contracts in the LLC's name, not your own; (4) keep the LLC's registration current with your state; and (5) ensure you have a proper operating agreement. Courts will pierce the corporate veil and hold members personally liable when these requirements are not met.
