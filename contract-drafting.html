Contract Law Education

Why Contract Quality Matters

A contract is a legally binding agreement between two or more parties. When properly drafted, it defines the rights and obligations of each party, establishes remedies for breach, and provides a clear framework for resolving disputes. When poorly drafted — or absent entirely — it leaves everything to interpretation, negotiation, and litigation.

Most contract disputes arise not because the parties had fundamentally different intentions, but because those intentions were never written down clearly. Ambiguous payment terms, undefined deliverables, missing deadlines, absent termination provisions, and inadequate remedies clauses are the most common sources of expensive contract litigation.

A well-drafted contract addresses what each party will do, when they will do it, how much will be paid and when, what happens if either party fails to perform, how disputes will be resolved, and how the agreement can be terminated. These provisions are not boilerplate — they are the document's functional core, and they must be tailored to the specific transaction.

What Makes a Contract Enforceable?

A contract is generally enforceable when there is an offer, acceptance of that offer, and consideration (something of value exchanged between the parties). Both parties must have the legal capacity to contract, and the subject matter must be lawful. Beyond those basics, enforceability depends on how clearly the terms are expressed. Vague terms produce disputes; clear terms produce compliance.

Verbal Agreements vs. Written Contracts

Many agreements are legally binding even without a written contract — but they are extraordinarily difficult to enforce. Without a written document, the terms of the agreement exist only in the parties' memories, which diverge over time and under financial pressure. Certain agreements — including those for the sale of real estate, agreements that cannot be performed within one year, and agreements for the sale of goods over $500 — are generally required by the Statute of Frauds to be in writing to be enforceable.

Who We Serve

Who Needs Professional Contract Drafting?

Anyone who exchanges services, products, or value with another party needs a contract. The question is whether that contract protects your interests or exposes you to unnecessary risk.

Freelancers and Consultants

If you provide services and get paid for them, you need a service agreement for every engagement. Without one, the scope of work, payment terms, revision rights, and termination are left undefined — and disputes become inevitable.

Small Business Owners

Every vendor relationship, customer engagement, employee hire, and strategic partnership involves contracts. Professionally drafted agreements reduce the disputes, protect the business, and create clear expectations for all parties.

Startups and Entrepreneurs

Early-stage businesses often operate on handshakes, email threads, and good faith. Founders who fail to put proper agreements in place — with co-founders, investors, employees, and customers — frequently face expensive disputes that threaten the business itself.

Real Estate Investors

Purchase agreements, assignment agreements, lease agreements, and seller financing documents are all contracts. Each transaction deserves a document that reflects its specific terms rather than a generic form that may not address the particulars of the deal.

Anyone Signing Someone Else's Contract

When the other party presents a contract for you to sign, you are being asked to accept their terms. Having that contract reviewed — and understanding what you are agreeing to — is the most important step before signing anything.

Technology and SaaS Businesses

Software products, licensing agreements, terms of service, and user agreements require specific provisions addressing intellectual property ownership, limitations of liability, subscription terms, and data handling. These are not generic documents.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What should every business contract include?

At minimum, a business contract should include the identity of the parties, a clear description of what each party will do, the payment terms (amount, due dates, method), the duration of the agreement, the termination provisions, what happens if either party breaches, a dispute resolution clause, and applicable law and venue. Missing any of these elements creates gaps that become disputes.

What is the difference between a mutual NDA and a one-way NDA?

A mutual NDA (also called a bilateral NDA) protects confidential information shared by both parties — common in business negotiations where both sides will be disclosing proprietary information. A one-way NDA (also called a unilateral NDA) protects only one party's confidential information — common in employment situations, vendor relationships, and due diligence processes where only one side is sharing sensitive information.

Do I need a new contract for each client or project?

A master service agreement defines the general terms of your business relationship with a client, and a statement of work or scope of work document defines the specifics of each project. This two-document structure allows you to establish core terms once and add project-specific details without renegotiating the whole agreement each time. We prepare both.

What is an indemnification clause and why does it matter?

An indemnification clause requires one party to compensate the other for certain losses or claims arising from the agreement or the indemnifying party's conduct. These clauses are among the most negotiated and most consequential provisions in commercial contracts. A broad indemnification clause can expose you to liability far beyond what you expected to assume. We draft indemnification provisions that are specific, balanced, and clearly limited to the agreed scope.

Can I use a contract template I found online?

Generic templates may address common situations but they rarely address the specifics of your transaction, your state's law, or your industry's particular requirements. They are a starting point, not a substitute for a properly drafted agreement. A contract prepared based on your actual terms and facts will protect you far better than a template you found online and modified yourself.
