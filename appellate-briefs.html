Appellate Practice Education

What Is the Appellate Process?

An appeal is a request to a higher court to review a decision made by a lower court. Unlike a trial, an appeal does not involve new evidence or witnesses — it is based entirely on the record from the lower court proceeding. The appellate court reviews the record to determine whether legal errors occurred that warrant reversal or modification of the lower court's ruling.

The appellate process is driven almost entirely by written briefs. The appellant (the party appealing) files an opening brief that identifies the errors that occurred in the lower court and argues why those errors require reversal. The appellee (the party defending the lower court's ruling) files a response brief. The appellant may then file a reply brief responding to the appellee's arguments. In some cases, other parties are permitted to file amicus curiae (friend of the court) briefs.

Appellate courts have specific and strict rules governing brief format, length, citation format, and filing procedures. These rules differ by court — federal courts of appeals follow the Federal Rules of Appellate Procedure, and each circuit has local rules; state appellate courts each have their own procedural rules. Briefs that do not comply with court rules are rejected or disregarded. The formatting requirements exist because appellate judges review enormous volumes of briefs and expect consistent presentation.

The Importance of Issue Identification

Appellate courts do not search the record for errors — they review only the issues the appellant raises in the brief. Issues not raised in the opening brief are generally waived. This means that identifying the right issues, stating them clearly in the issue-presented section, and arguing them in the body of the brief is the most consequential work in appellate practice. We prepare briefs that organize and present the arguments you identify in a structure appellate judges expect.

Pro Se Appellate Filers

Many individuals choose to represent themselves in appellate proceedings — particularly in civil appeals, family law appeals, and administrative agency appeals. The challenge is that appellate courts hold all filers — represented and pro se — to the same formatting and procedural standards. A brief that is improperly formatted, that cites the record incorrectly, or that raises arguments in the wrong section will be disadvantaged. Professionally prepared appellate documents give pro se filers a meaningful advantage.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the standard of review and why does it matter?

The standard of review is the legal standard an appellate court applies when evaluating the lower court's decision. Different types of decisions receive different levels of deference. Questions of law are reviewed de novo — the appellate court decides the legal question fresh, without deference to the lower court. Factual findings are reviewed for clear error — the appellate court will reverse only if convinced that the lower court's factual finding was clearly wrong. Discretionary decisions are reviewed for abuse of discretion — the most deferential standard, reversed only if the lower court acted outside its reasonable range of choices. Identifying and arguing the correct standard of review is essential to every appellate brief.

What is the deadline to file a notice of appeal?

Appellate deadlines are jurisdictionally critical — missing the deadline to file a notice of appeal almost always results in dismissal of the appeal for lack of jurisdiction, with very limited exceptions. In federal civil cases, the deadline is generally 30 days from entry of judgment (60 days if the United States or a U.S. agency is a party). In federal criminal cases, it is 14 days. State court deadlines vary widely. Contact us immediately after receiving an adverse judgment — we treat appeal deadlines as the most urgent aspect of any appellate matter.

Can I raise new arguments on appeal that I did not raise in the trial court?

Generally, no. Appellate courts apply the preservation doctrine, which provides that issues not raised in the trial court are waived on appeal. To preserve an issue for appeal, the party must have raised it in the trial court and obtained a ruling. There are limited exceptions — issues involving jurisdiction, plain error in criminal cases, and issues that were not available at the time of trial — but the exceptions are narrow. This means that issue identification on appeal requires careful review of the trial court record to identify which issues were raised and ruled upon below.

How long should an appellate brief be?

Appellate courts impose strict word count or page limits on briefs. In the federal courts of appeals, the standard limit is 13,000 words for an opening or response brief and 6,500 words for a reply brief (or 30 and 15 pages, respectively, if the brief does not include a certificate of compliance). State court limits vary. Every brief we prepare includes a certification of compliance with the applicable word or page limit. Briefs must also comply with font, margin, and spacing requirements.

What happens at oral argument and do I need to attend?

Oral argument is the opportunity for each side to present its position to the appellate panel and answer the judges' questions. Not all appeals receive oral argument — courts may decide cases on the briefs alone. When oral argument is granted, it is typically limited to 10-20 minutes per side. The court has already read the briefs before oral argument, so argument is most valuable for answering questions and addressing the court's concerns. We do not represent parties at oral argument — our services are limited to document preparation. Oral argument preparation coaching is available through our legal education services.
