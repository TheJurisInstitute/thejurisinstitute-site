How a Living Trust Protects Your Assets

October 17, 2026 8 min read
← Back to Blog

Many people assume a Last Will and Testament is all they need for estate planning. A will is essential, but it has significant limitations — chief among them that it must pass through probate, a court-supervised process that is public, time-consuming, and expensive. A revocable living trust solves those problems while giving you greater control over how your assets pass to your heirs.

What a Living Trust Is

A revocable living trust is a legal document you create during your lifetime. You transfer ownership of your assets — real estate, bank accounts, investments — into the trust, but you remain the trustee and continue to manage and use those assets exactly as you did before. The trust holds the assets; you control the trust.

You name a successor trustee who takes over management of the trust if you become incapacitated or when you die. You also name beneficiaries who receive the assets after your death, under whatever terms and conditions you specify.

As a revocable trust, you can change it at any time during your lifetime — add or remove assets, change beneficiaries, change the successor trustee, or revoke it entirely. It becomes irrevocable at your death.

The Central Advantage: Avoiding Probate

Assets held in a properly funded living trust do not pass through probate. This matters for several reasons:

Speed: Probate can take six months to two years, during which time your heirs may have limited access to assets. A trust typically allows distribution within weeks.

Cost: Probate involves court fees, executor fees, and often attorney fees. In some states (California, Florida), these fees are set by statute as a percentage of the estate's gross value — not its net value — and can be substantial. A trust avoids all of these costs.

Privacy: A will that goes through probate becomes a public record. Anyone can read it, including the details of what you owned and who inherited it. A trust is entirely private.

Multi-state property: If you own real estate in more than one state, your estate may need to go through probate in each state where real estate is located (called ancillary probate). A trust that holds real estate in multiple states avoids this entirely.

Incapacity Planning

A living trust does something a will cannot: it plans for incapacity as well as death. If you become unable to manage your own affairs — due to a stroke, dementia, or serious illness — your successor trustee can step in immediately and manage the trust assets without court involvement. A will takes effect only at death; it has no role in incapacity planning.

For this reason, a living trust is typically paired with a Durable Power of Attorney for assets not yet transferred into the trust, and a Healthcare Directive for medical decisions.

A living trust is only as effective as its funding. A trust that exists on paper but holds none of your assets still requires all of those assets to pass through probate. The trust must actually hold the assets — real estate deeded into the trust, bank accounts re-titled, investment accounts transferred — to accomplish anything.

Controlled Distribution to Heirs

A trust gives you precise control over when and how beneficiaries receive assets, in ways a simple will cannot match:

  • Hold assets for a minor child until they reach a specified age (25, 30, in stages)
  • Provide for a spouse during their lifetime, with the remaining assets passing to your children at the spouse's death
  • Protect an heir with special needs without disqualifying them from government benefits (a Special Needs Trust)
  • Protect a beneficiary's inheritance from their creditors or divorce proceedings
  • Stagger distributions to prevent a beneficiary from squandering a large inheritance

What a Living Trust Does Not Do

A revocable living trust does not provide asset protection from your own creditors during your lifetime — because you retain control, your creditors can reach the trust assets. Asset protection from creditors while you are alive requires an irrevocable trust structure.

A living trust also does not reduce estate taxes on its own. Federal estate tax planning requires separate strategies — an irrevocable trust, a Spousal Lifetime Access Trust (SLAT), a Charitable Remainder Trust, or others — depending on the size of your estate.

Do You Need a Living Trust?

A living trust is most valuable if you:

  • Own real estate, especially in more than one state
  • Have minor children or heirs who should not receive an outright lump sum
  • Have a significant estate and want to avoid probate costs
  • Value privacy
  • Have a blended family or complex family dynamics
  • Are concerned about incapacity planning

For a young person with modest assets, a simple will paired with updated beneficiary designations may be sufficient for now. Estate plans should grow with your circumstances.
