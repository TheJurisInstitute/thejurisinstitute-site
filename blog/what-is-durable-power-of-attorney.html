What Is a Durable Power of Attorney and Why You Need One

October 24, 2026 7 min read
← Back to Blog

Of all the legal documents in a complete estate plan, the Durable Power of Attorney for finances may be the most overlooked — and the one whose absence causes the most immediate hardship for families. It has nothing to do with death. It plans for something more common: the possibility that you become temporarily or permanently unable to manage your own financial affairs.

What a Durable Power of Attorney Does

A Power of Attorney is a legal document through which you (the "principal") give another person (the "agent" or "attorney-in-fact") the legal authority to act on your behalf. A Durable Power of Attorney includes specific language providing that the document remains effective even if you become incapacitated — which is precisely when you need it most.

A non-durable Power of Attorney automatically terminates if you become mentally incapacitated, which defeats its purpose for incapacity planning. Always use a durable version for estate planning purposes.

What Your Agent Can Do

The scope of authority you grant in the document determines what your agent can do. A broad, general Power of Attorney might grant your agent authority to:

  • Manage your bank accounts — make deposits, pay bills, write checks
  • Buy, sell, or manage real estate
  • File tax returns and interact with the IRS on your behalf
  • Manage investment accounts and make investment decisions
  • Apply for government benefits (Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid)
  • Make gifts (typically limited to annual exclusion amounts)
  • Manage business interests
  • Borrow money or enter into contracts

You can also create a limited Power of Attorney that grants authority only for specific transactions — for example, authorizing an agent to sell a specific piece of property while you are traveling or incapacitated.

What a Durable Power of Attorney Does Not Cover

A financial Durable Power of Attorney covers financial and property decisions. It does not cover healthcare decisions — those require a separate document, a Healthcare Power of Attorney (also called a Healthcare Proxy or Medical Power of Attorney), which designates someone to make medical decisions on your behalf.

A complete incapacity plan includes both:

  • A Durable Power of Attorney for finances
  • A Healthcare Power of Attorney for medical decisions
  • A Living Will or Advance Healthcare Directive stating your wishes regarding life-sustaining treatment

What Happens Without One

If you become incapacitated without a Durable Power of Attorney in place, your family has no automatic legal authority to manage your finances — even a spouse does not automatically have authority over accounts held in your name alone. To gain that authority, they must petition the court to appoint a conservator (sometimes called a guardian of the estate).

Conservatorship proceedings are:

  • Expensive — court filing fees, attorney fees, physician examinations, and ongoing reporting costs
  • Time-consuming — the process takes months, during which bills go unpaid and decisions are delayed
  • Public — the proceeding and its findings become public record
  • Ongoing — a conservator must file annual accountings with the court and seek court approval for certain transactions

A Durable Power of Attorney is one of the least expensive legal documents you can prepare and one of the most valuable in a crisis. The cost of not having one — a conservatorship proceeding — can run to tens of thousands of dollars and months of court proceedings.

Choosing Your Agent

Your agent will have broad authority over your finances. This is an enormous amount of trust to place in another person. Choose carefully:

  • Trustworthiness: Financial elder abuse most commonly involves someone with a power of attorney. Choose someone whose honesty you are absolutely certain of.
  • Competence: Financial management requires judgment and organization. Choose someone capable of those tasks.
  • Availability: An agent who lives far away or has demanding work and family commitments may not be able to respond quickly in a crisis.
  • Name a successor agent: If your primary agent cannot serve — because they predeceased you, became incapacitated themselves, or declined the role — you need a backup.

Springing vs. Immediate Powers

A Durable Power of Attorney can be either "immediate" (effective the moment you sign it) or "springing" (effective only upon your incapacity, as certified by a physician). Most estate planning attorneys favor immediate powers for practical reasons: banks and financial institutions are sometimes reluctant to accept a springing power because proving incapacity requires a physician's certification that may be difficult to obtain quickly in a crisis.

Acceptance by Banks and Financial Institutions

One practical challenge with Powers of Attorney is that banks and financial institutions sometimes refuse to honor them — claiming the document is outdated, using non-standard language, or simply as institutional policy. Many states have addressed this with "statutory short form" powers of attorney that financial institutions are required to accept. Using a form that complies with your state's statutory form requirements reduces the risk of refusal.
