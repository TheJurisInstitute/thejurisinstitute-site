What Is a Prenuptial Agreement and What Should It Include?

November 15, 2026 10 min read
← Back to Blog

You’re planning the most exciting chapter of your life, and the last thing you want to think about is what happens if things don’t work out. That’s understandable. But a prenuptial agreement — often called a prenup — is not a prediction of failure. It is a practical document that protects both of you, clarifies your financial expectations going into the marriage, and can actually make your relationship stronger by putting everything on the table before the wedding day. Knowing what a prenuptial agreement is, what it should include, and when it makes sense is information every couple deserves to have before they sign a marriage license.

What Is a Prenuptial Agreement?

A prenuptial agreement is a written contract entered into by two people before they get married. In it, the spouses-to-be agree in advance on how their property, debts, and financial rights will be handled — both during the marriage and in the event of divorce, separation, or death. The agreement is signed before the wedding and takes effect the moment the marriage is solemnized.

The legal term varies by state. Some states use “antenuptial agreement.” Others use “premarital agreement.” The most common names in everyday usage are prenup and prenuptial agreement. Whatever the terminology, the core purpose is the same: to give both parties certainty and control over issues that family courts would otherwise decide according to state default rules if the marriage ends.

Prenuptial agreements are governed by state law, and the requirements for a valid, enforceable agreement vary from state to state. Most states have adopted some version of the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act, which provides a framework for what can and cannot be included, what makes an agreement enforceable, and what grounds can be used to challenge one. Because state rules differ in important ways, a prenuptial agreement needs to be tailored to the law of the state where you reside and where you plan to marry.

What a Prenuptial Agreement Can Cover

A well-drafted prenuptial agreement can address a wide range of financial and property matters. The following are among the most common and important provisions couples include.

Separate Property Protection

One of the primary functions of a prenup is to identify what each person owns before the marriage and confirm that it will remain their separate property if the marriage ends. Without a prenuptial agreement, property that was clearly yours before the wedding can sometimes become entangled with marital assets — especially in long marriages where funds get commingled. A prenup draws a clear line. It lists the assets, real estate, bank accounts, retirement savings, and business interests each party is bringing into the marriage and states that those items will not be subject to division upon divorce.

Business Ownership and Business Interests

If you own a business — or have a stake in a family business — a prenuptial agreement is one of the most effective tools for protecting it. Without a prenup, your spouse may be entitled to a share of the appreciation in your business’s value that occurred during the marriage, even if they had nothing to do with running it. A well-crafted prenup can protect your ownership interest, limit any claim to business assets, and ensure that a divorce proceeding does not threaten the viability of a company that employs other people and serves customers.

Inheritance and Family Assets

If you expect to receive an inheritance — from a parent, grandparent, or other relative — a prenuptial agreement can confirm that any inherited assets will remain your separate property and will not be treated as marital property subject to division. This is particularly important for families with significant real estate holdings, family trusts, or multi-generational businesses. Many families actually encourage — or even require — that heirs enter into prenuptial agreements before marriage specifically to protect inherited wealth from the risk of divorce.

Debt Allocation

Marriage can expose you to your spouse’s debts in ways that surprise people. A prenuptial agreement can specify that debts each person brings into the marriage remain their own responsibility. This is especially important when one partner is entering the marriage with significant student loans, credit card debt, a business with liabilities, or other obligations. Without clarity in writing, you may find yourself held responsible for debts that were never yours.

Spousal Support (Alimony)

Many couples use a prenuptial agreement to address spousal support — also called alimony or spousal maintenance. The agreement can set a fixed amount, establish a formula based on the length of the marriage, limit the duration of support, or waive spousal support entirely. Courts in most states will enforce reasonable spousal support provisions in a prenup, though they may decline to enforce provisions that would leave one spouse destitute or that were clearly unconscionable at the time of signing. This is one area where the specific language and circumstances of signing matter a great deal.

Property Division Rules

Beyond pre-marital assets, a prenuptial agreement can establish rules for how property acquired during the marriage will be divided if the couple divorces. For instance, the agreement might specify that the marital home — which the couple will purchase together — will be sold and proceeds split equally, or it might give one spouse the right to buy out the other’s interest. It can address how retirement accounts accumulated during the marriage will be treated, how investment accounts will be divided, and what happens to jointly acquired real estate.

Financial Responsibilities During the Marriage

Some couples use a prenuptial agreement to address how finances will be managed during the marriage itself — whether both spouses will contribute to household expenses, how joint accounts will work, how major financial decisions will be made, and what each person’s financial responsibilities will be. These provisions are less commonly litigated than divorce-related provisions, but they can be useful for couples who are coming in with very different financial backgrounds or expectations.

A note on estate planning: A prenuptial agreement can coordinate with your estate plan by confirming that each spouse waives any right to inherit the other’s separate property, allowing each person to leave their own assets to children from a prior relationship or other beneficiaries. This is especially valuable in blended families where both spouses have children from previous marriages.

What a Prenuptial Agreement Cannot Cover

Prenuptial agreements are powerful documents, but they have real limits. Courts will not enforce every provision a couple might want to include, and some subjects are simply off the table.

Child custody and child support. A prenuptial agreement cannot predetermine custody arrangements or child support obligations for children who have not yet been born. Courts will not enforce these provisions because the law requires custody and support decisions to be made in the best interest of the child at the time the issue arises — not locked in before the child exists. If you and your spouse later divorce with children involved, a family court will make custody and support decisions based on the circumstances at that time, regardless of what your prenup says.

Encouraging divorce. An agreement that actually incentivizes one party to seek a divorce — for example, by offering a large payment triggered by filing — will generally be unenforceable. Courts view such provisions as contrary to public policy.

Non-financial personal matters. A prenuptial agreement is a financial document. Provisions about where you will live, how often you will visit in-laws, who does the housework, or other personal lifestyle choices are generally unenforceable. Courts simply will not police those kinds of arrangements.

Waiving rights to public benefits. If a provision would force one spouse to rely on public assistance rather than spousal support, courts in most states will refuse to enforce it. The state has an interest in not creating public benefit dependency through contract.

When Should You Consider a Prenuptial Agreement?

Prenuptial agreements are not just for the wealthy, and they are not a sign of distrust. They are a practical planning tool for a wide range of couples. Here are some of the most common situations where a prenup makes particular sense.

You own real estate, a business, or significant investments. Any time one partner is bringing meaningful assets into a marriage, a prenup is worth serious consideration. The more complex your financial picture, the more valuable it is to establish clear ownership rules in writing.

You have children from a prior relationship. If you want to ensure that specific assets or an inheritance pass to your children rather than to a future ex-spouse, a prenuptial agreement — coordinated with your estate plan — is essential.

One or both of you has significant debt. A prenup can protect each party from becoming responsible for the other’s pre-marital debts, which is especially important when student loans, business liabilities, or other obligations are involved.

There is a significant disparity in income or wealth. When one partner earns substantially more or has more assets, a prenuptial agreement gives both parties clarity on what the financial relationship will look like — and protects the higher-earning or higher-asset partner while also providing reasonable protection for the other.

You are entering a second marriage. People remarrying later in life frequently have more complex financial situations — retirement savings, real estate, business interests, and obligations to children from a first marriage. A prenuptial agreement is particularly valuable in this context.

You simply want financial clarity. Even couples without complicated financial situations benefit from the conversations that a prenuptial agreement requires. Going through the process of disclosing your assets, debts, and financial expectations to each other — and working through any disagreements before the wedding — builds the kind of financial transparency that is associated with stronger, longer-lasting marriages.

What Makes a Prenuptial Agreement Enforceable?

A prenuptial agreement that fails to meet legal requirements can be thrown out entirely, leaving both parties unprotected. Courts look at several factors when deciding whether to enforce a prenup.

It must be in writing and signed. An oral prenuptial agreement is not enforceable. The agreement must be a written document signed by both parties before the wedding.

Both parties must fully disclose their finances. Courts will not enforce a prenuptial agreement if one party hid assets or debts from the other. Full financial disclosure — a detailed accounting of what each person owns and owes — is a requirement in virtually every state. If you discover after a divorce that your spouse concealed significant assets before you signed the prenup, you may be able to challenge the agreement in court.

Both parties must sign voluntarily. An agreement signed under duress, pressure, or coercion is not enforceable. This is one of the most important reasons to allow adequate time before the wedding. Courts have set aside prenups that were presented to one spouse just days before the ceremony, on the theory that the pressures of an imminent wedding prevented truly voluntary agreement. Best practice is to have the agreement fully negotiated and signed at least 30 days before the wedding.

The agreement must not be unconscionable. Courts retain the power to refuse enforcement of provisions that are grossly unfair at the time of signing. What constitutes unconscionability varies by state, but as a general rule, a one-sided agreement that leaves one party with almost nothing is at higher risk of being challenged than a balanced agreement that reflects each party’s reasonable interests.

The correct legal formalities must be followed. Depending on your state, a prenuptial agreement may need to be witnessed, notarized, or meet specific formatting requirements. Using a document that was prepared for the wrong state — or one that lacks the required execution formalities — can result in the entire agreement being unenforceable when you need it most.

Timing matters more than most people realize: Courts scrutinize prenuptial agreements signed under time pressure. If the agreement was handed to one party the week before the wedding, with significant social and financial arrangements already in place, a court may find that the signing was not truly voluntary. Build in enough time for both parties to review the document carefully, ask questions, and negotiate any terms before signing.
