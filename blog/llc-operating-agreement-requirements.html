What Your LLC Operating Agreement Must Include

September 5, 2026 8 min read
← Back to Blog

When you form an LLC, the state only asks you to file Articles of Organization — a brief, public document that establishes the basic existence of your company. But the document that actually governs how your LLC operates is the Operating Agreement, and most states don't require you to file it anywhere. It sits in your records and runs your company in the background.

Many business owners skip the Operating Agreement entirely, use a generic one-page template they found online, or treat it as a formality rather than a real document. All three approaches create serious problems down the road. Here is what a proper Operating Agreement must address — and why each provision matters.

The Basics: Identity and Structure

Every Operating Agreement starts with the foundational information that identifies and defines the LLC:

  • Legal name of the LLC exactly as registered with the state
  • Principal place of business and registered agent information
  • Purpose of the business — some agreements use broad language ("any lawful purpose"), while others specify the business type
  • Duration — perpetual existence or a defined end date
  • Governing state law

Membership: Ownership, Contributions, and Percentages

The Operating Agreement is where ownership is formally defined. This section must cover:

  • The name of each member (owner)
  • Each member's ownership percentage or number of units
  • Capital contributions — what each member put in (cash, property, services) and when
  • Whether additional capital contributions can be required and under what circumstances
  • Whether membership interests are represented by certificates

Without clear ownership documentation in the Operating Agreement, disputes about who owns what percentage of the company are extraordinarily difficult to resolve after the fact.

Management: Member-Managed vs. Manager-Managed

One of the most important structural decisions in an LLC is how it will be managed. You must choose one of two models:

Member-managed: All members participate in day-to-day management and have authority to bind the company. This is common for single-member LLCs and small partnerships where all owners are active in the business.

Manager-managed: One or more designated managers (who may or may not be members) run the company. Other members are passive investors who don't participate in daily operations. This structure is essential for LLCs with investors who want ownership stakes without management responsibility.

The Operating Agreement must specify who the manager or managers are, what authority they have, what decisions require member approval, and how managers are appointed or removed.

Voting Rights and Decision-Making

The agreement needs clear rules for how decisions are made:

  • What percentage of ownership constitutes a quorum for a vote
  • What vote threshold is required for ordinary business decisions (typically majority)
  • What decisions require a supermajority or unanimous consent — major asset sales, taking on significant debt, adding new members, amending the Operating Agreement itself
  • How meetings are called and noticed
  • Whether written consents in lieu of meetings are allowed

Profit and Loss Allocations and Distributions

How money flows to the members must be spelled out precisely:

  • How profits and losses are allocated among members (typically proportional to ownership percentage, but can be customized)
  • When distributions are made and how the amount is determined
  • Whether any member is guaranteed a minimum distribution
  • Priority of distributions if members have different classes of ownership
  • Tax distributions — in a pass-through entity, members owe income tax on their share of profits whether or not they actually receive cash, so many agreements require the LLC to distribute enough cash to cover members' tax liability

Without a distribution provision, members can be stuck paying income tax on profits that the LLC reinvests and never distributes to them — a cash flow problem that catches many first-time LLC owners by surprise.

Transfer Restrictions: What Happens When a Member Wants Out

This is where many generic templates fall dangerously short. You need detailed provisions covering:

  • Right of first refusal: Before a member can sell their interest to a third party, must they offer it to the other members first?
  • Consent requirements: Can a member transfer their interest without the other members' approval?
  • Buy-sell provisions: What happens if a member dies, becomes disabled, files for bankruptcy, or is subject to a divorce proceeding? How is the interest valued and who can buy it?
  • Deadlock provisions: In a 50/50 LLC, what happens when the two members can't agree on a major decision?

Dissolution

Every Operating Agreement should address how the company ends:

  • What events trigger dissolution (unanimous member vote, death of the last member, etc.)
  • How assets are liquidated and liabilities paid
  • How remaining assets are distributed to members

Why a Template Isn't Enough

A one-size-fits-all Operating Agreement may satisfy the bare minimum in your state, but it won't address the specifics of your business, your ownership structure, or your state's particular default rules. In fact, your state's default LLC rules (what applies when your Operating Agreement is silent) may be exactly the opposite of what you want.
