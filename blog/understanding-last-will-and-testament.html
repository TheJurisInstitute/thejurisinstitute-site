Understanding a Last Will and Testament

September 12, 2026 7 min read
← Back to Blog

A Last Will and Testament is the most fundamental document in any estate plan. It is the legal instrument through which you direct how your property is distributed after you die, name who will manage that process, and — crucially — name a guardian for any minor children. Yet most Americans die without one, leaving those decisions to state law and the courts.

Here is what a will does, what it cannot do, and what it must contain to be legally valid in most states.

What a Will Actually Does

A will accomplishes several things simultaneously:

  • Names an executor (sometimes called a personal representative) to manage your estate, pay your debts, and distribute your assets
  • Directs who receives your probate assets — property that does not pass automatically by contract (like life insurance) or by joint ownership
  • Names a guardian for your minor children if both parents die — one of the most important decisions a parent can put in writing
  • Names a trustee to manage assets left to minor beneficiaries until they reach a certain age
  • Expresses your wishes about funeral and burial arrangements (though this is not legally binding in most states)

What a Will Cannot Do

A common misconception is that a will controls everything you own. In reality, many assets pass outside the will entirely, and those designations override whatever the will says:

  • Life insurance proceeds go to the named beneficiary on the policy, not your estate
  • Retirement accounts (401(k), IRA, pension) pass to the designated beneficiary on file with the plan administrator
  • Jointly owned property with right of survivorship passes automatically to the surviving co-owner
  • Bank accounts with a payable-on-death (POD) designation pass directly to the named person
  • Assets held in a trust pass according to the trust document, not the will

This means your will may only control a fraction of your total estate. Beneficiary designations on financial accounts are often more important than the will itself — and they're often years out of date. Review them every time your life changes.

Basic Requirements for a Valid Will

Will requirements vary by state, but most states require all of the following:

  • Age: The person making the will (the "testator") must be at least 18 years old (some states allow younger individuals who are married or in military service)
  • Mental capacity: The testator must understand the nature of making a will, the extent of their property, who their natural heirs are, and how the will distributes property
  • Writing: The will must be in writing (oral wills are recognized in very few, narrow circumstances)
  • Signature: The testator must sign the will, or direct someone else to sign it in their presence if they cannot physically sign
  • Witnesses: Most states require two witnesses who watch the testator sign and then sign themselves; witnesses typically must not be beneficiaries under the will

Some states also recognize holographic wills — handwritten wills signed by the testator that do not require witnesses. However, a holographic will is not recognized in all states, and even in states that recognize them, they are prone to challenges.

What Happens Without a Will

If you die without a will — called dying "intestate" — your state's intestacy laws determine who receives your property. These laws follow a rigid formula based on family relationships: spouse first, then children, then parents, then siblings, and so on. The formula does not account for:

  • The person you've been living with for years who is not your legal spouse
  • A stepchild you raised but never legally adopted
  • A close friend you want to receive a specific item
  • A charity you care about
  • Any family dynamics — a distant relative you've never met may inherit before a close friend

And without a will naming a guardian, a judge will decide who raises your children.

A Will Is the Starting Point, Not the Complete Plan

For many people, a will combined with properly updated beneficiary designations is sufficient. For others — those with significant assets, blended families, minor children, or specific wishes about how and when beneficiaries receive their inheritance — a living trust provides greater flexibility and avoids the cost and delay of probate.
