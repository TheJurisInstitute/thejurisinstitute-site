How to Respond to a Debt Collection Lawsuit

November 1, 2026 8 min read
← Back to Blog

Knowing how to respond to a debt collection lawsuit is one of the most time-sensitive legal situations you can face as an individual or small business owner. When a summons and complaint arrive in the mail — or are handed to you by a process server — the clock starts ticking immediately. How you handle the next few days can determine whether a creditor is able to garnish your wages, freeze your bank account, or place a lien on your property. The encouraging reality is that responding is well within reach of someone without a legal background. What it requires is understanding the process and having a correctly prepared legal document filed with the court before your deadline expires.

What the Lawsuit Papers Actually Mean

When a creditor or debt buyer files suit against you, the court issues two documents together: a Summons and a Complaint.

The Summons is the official court notice that you have been sued. It names the court, the parties, the case number, and — most critically — the deadline by which you must file a written response. The Complaint is the substantive document: it sets out what the plaintiff claims you owe, the legal theory behind the claim (typically breach of contract or account stated), and the dollar amount sought.

Read both documents carefully and completely. Confirm that your name is spelled correctly and that the address where you were served is accurate. Write down the case number, because it belongs on every document you file going forward. Then mark your response deadline. That date is not a suggestion — it is the single most important number in the entire lawsuit.

How to Respond to a Debt Collection Lawsuit, Step by Step

Step 1 — Count Your Days Precisely

Every state sets its own deadline for filing a written Answer after a defendant is served with a lawsuit. Common windows are 20, 21, 28, or 30 days, but they vary. The Summons will often state the deadline, but you should verify it against your state’s civil procedure rules or ask the clerk of court’s public information office. The deadline runs from the date you were served — not the date on the document, not the date you found it in your mailbox, but the date of actual service. Missing this deadline by even a single day can result in a default judgment being entered against you.

Step 2 — Gather Every Record Related to the Debt

Before drafting your response, collect everything you have that touches the account in question: credit card agreements, billing statements, bank records, and any correspondence from the original creditor or from collection agencies. You are looking for two things: whether you actually owe the amount claimed, and whether the entity suing you has the legal right to do so.

Debt collection lawsuits are frequently filed not by original creditors but by debt buyers — companies that purchase portfolios of defaulted accounts for fractions of the original balance. These buyers often have incomplete records. Accounts may have passed through several hands, with errors accumulating at each transfer. The amount claimed may include fees that were never properly disclosed. In some cases, the account does not even belong to the named defendant.

Step 3 — Check the Statute of Limitations

Every state imposes a deadline on how long a creditor has to sue for an unpaid debt. This window, called the statute of limitations, typically runs three to six years for credit card debt depending on the state, though some states apply longer periods to written contracts. If the debt is older than the applicable limitations period — measured from the date of the last payment or the date the account went into default, depending on your state’s rule — you may have a complete legal defense. This defense does not make the debt disappear, but it can bar the creditor from collecting it through a lawsuit.

Step 4 — Prepare and File Your Written Answer

Your Answer is a formal legal document in which you respond to each numbered paragraph in the Complaint. For each allegation, you either admit it, deny it, or state that you lack sufficient information to admit or deny it. At the end of the response section, you list your affirmative defenses — legal grounds that can defeat or limit the plaintiff’s claim even if some or all of the underlying facts are true.

Common affirmative defenses raised in debt collection cases include the following:

  • The statute of limitations has expired on the alleged debt
  • Lack of standing — the plaintiff cannot prove ownership of the account
  • Payment or partial settlement has already been made
  • The amount claimed is incorrect or includes unauthorized charges
  • The debt was discharged in a prior bankruptcy proceeding
  • Identity error — the account does not belong to this defendant
  • Failure to provide required notices under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act

Once the Answer is complete, you file it with the clerk of the civil court identified on the Summons. Most courts charge a filing fee, which varies by jurisdiction. You must also serve a copy on the plaintiff’s attorney, typically by first-class mail, as required by your state’s civil procedure rules.

What happens if you do not respond: If you fail to file a written Answer by the deadline on your Summons, the plaintiff can move for a default judgment — meaning the court rules in the creditor’s favor without ever hearing your side. Once a judgment is entered, collection can begin immediately. In most states that means wage garnishment (a percentage of your paycheck withheld by your employer and sent to the creditor), bank levies (funds seized directly from your account), and liens recorded against real property you own. A judgment also appears on your credit record. Filing even a basic Answer that denies the allegations keeps the case open and forces the plaintiff to actually prove its claim in court.

What to Include in a Properly Drafted Answer

Courts have specific formatting requirements for documents filed in civil cases. A properly prepared Answer for a debt collection case typically contains the following components, in order:

  • A caption identifying the court, the parties, and the case number, with the title "Defendant’s Answer to Plaintiff’s Complaint"
  • An introductory paragraph identifying who is filing and in what capacity
  • Numbered responses, one for each paragraph of the Complaint — admit, deny, or lack of knowledge
  • A section labeled "Affirmative Defenses" listing each defense the defendant intends to raise
  • A jury demand, if applicable and desired, placed before the signature block
  • Signature, printed name, mailing address, phone number, and the date of filing

Formatting errors — wrong margins, missing case numbers, incorrect document titles — can result in the clerk rejecting the document. Local courts sometimes have additional requirements for pro se filings, which is the term for individuals representing themselves. Checking the court’s self-help resources or public information desk before filing is a reasonable step.

After You File: Discovery and What Comes Next

Once your Answer is on file, the case moves into pre-trial proceedings. The plaintiff may serve written discovery on you: interrogatories (written questions you must answer under oath), requests for production of documents, and requests for admission. You have the same right to send discovery to the plaintiff — and this is often where debt collection cases become complicated for the suing party.

Plaintiffs must be able to produce the original credit agreement, a complete account history, and documentation of the chain of title proving that the plaintiff actually owns the debt and has standing to sue. Many debt buyers simply do not have these records for accounts that are old or have been resold multiple times. Demanding that documentation through discovery is a legitimate defense strategy that can lead to a voluntary dismissal or a negotiated settlement for far less than the amount originally claimed.

Learning how to respond to a debt collection lawsuit is not just about the initial filing. It is about understanding that once your Answer is in, the burden shifts back to the plaintiff to prove its case — and that burden is often heavier than the lawsuit papers make it appear.

Responding to a Debt Lawsuit Without an Attorney

Most courts permit individuals to represent themselves in civil matters, including debt collection cases. Self-representation is called appearing "pro se," and clerks of court are generally accustomed to assisting pro se filers with procedural questions — though they cannot provide legal advice on strategy or the substance of your defenses.

The most effective approach for most individuals facing a debt collection lawsuit is to have a correctly prepared Answer — one that properly raises every applicable defense — and to file it on time. A document that is timely, properly captioned, and raises legitimate defenses gives you a genuine opportunity to resolve the case on favorable terms. A document that is late, missing defenses, or formatted incorrectly can undermine an otherwise strong position.
