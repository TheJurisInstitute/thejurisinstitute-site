Will, Deed or Trust: Which Do You Need?

November 22, 2026 6 min read
← Back to Blog

Families ask us some version of the same question every week: “Do I need a will, a new deed or a trust?” It is a fair question, because all three deal with who ends up with your property, and the names get used loosely. But they are different documents that do different jobs, and choosing the wrong one, or assuming one covers what only another can do, is one of the most common gaps in a family’s plan.

This short guide explains what each document does and does not do, then walks through five common family situations. It is general education, not legal advice, and the rules vary by state, so check your state’s requirements before you act.

The Short Answer

A will gives instructions that take effect after you die, and it usually goes through probate court. A deed transfers ownership of a specific piece of real estate, usually right away. A living trust holds assets you put into it and lets a successor trustee manage and distribute them, often without probate, both if you die and if you become unable to manage your own affairs.

Many families end up with more than one of these, because each fills a gap the others leave.

What a Will Does, and What It Does Not

A last will and testament names an executor to settle your estate, says who receives the property that is in your name alone, and, for parents of minor children, is where you nominate a guardian. That last point matters: if you have young children, a will is usually the document that lets you name who you want to raise them.

A will does not avoid probate. The executor generally has to take the will through the probate process before property can be distributed, and the will becomes a public court record in most places. A will also does not control assets that pass another way, such as life insurance and retirement accounts with named beneficiaries, accounts with payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designations, and property owned jointly with a right of survivorship. Those go to the named beneficiary or the surviving owner regardless of what the will says.

Check your beneficiary designations. Many family estates pass mostly through beneficiary forms, not the will. An outdated form can send an account to an ex-spouse or to someone who has died, even if your will says otherwise.

What a Deed Does, and What It Does Not

A deed transfers ownership of real estate. Families most often use one to add a spouse to the title, to move a home into a living trust, or to change how co-owners hold title, for example as joint tenants with right of survivorship. A deed usually takes effect when it is signed, notarized and recorded with the county.

Many states also allow a transfer-on-death deed (sometimes called a beneficiary deed). You keep full ownership during your life, and the property passes to the person you name when you die, without probate. Not every state offers this option, and the rules for signing and recording it are strict, so confirm that your state allows it before relying on one.

A deed only covers the property it describes. It does not name a guardian, does not deal with your bank accounts or personal belongings, and adding someone to your title now can have consequences of its own, including giving them an ownership interest today and possible gift tax reporting. Think it through before you sign.

What a Living Trust Does, and What It Does Not

A revocable living trust is a document you create, usually naming yourself as trustee while you are alive. You transfer assets into it, and you keep full control. If you become incapacitated, your successor trustee can step in without a court guardianship proceeding. When you die, the successor trustee distributes the trust’s assets according to your instructions, usually without probate and without a public court record.

A trust also lets you control timing. Instead of a child receiving everything at eighteen, the trust can hold their share and release it in stages, or for education and support.

The catch is that a trust only controls what it owns. A home that is never deeded into the trust, or an account that is never retitled, can still end up in probate. Most people with a trust also sign a short “pour-over” will that sends anything left outside the trust into it.

Which Do You Need? Five Common Family Situations

1. You have young children

Start with a will, because it is usually where you nominate a guardian. If you want a child’s inheritance managed until they are older, consider a trust as well, either a living trust or a trust created by your will.

2. You own a home and want to keep it out of probate

A living trust, with a new deed moving the home into it, is the most common approach. In states that allow it, a transfer-on-death deed can be a simpler option for a single property.

3. You want to add your spouse to the title

That is a deed. Before you sign, decide how you will hold title together, because that choice controls what happens to the home when one of you dies.

4. You have a blended family, or a beneficiary who needs help managing money

A trust gives you the most control over who receives what and when, and it can protect a share for children from an earlier marriage.

5. Most of what you own already has a beneficiary named

A simple will plus up-to-date beneficiary designations may cover you. Review every account’s beneficiary form, and keep a will in place for anything that does not pass by designation.

How They Work Together

For many families the answer is not one document but a small set: a will (or a pour-over will with a trust), the deeds needed to put real estate where the plan expects it, and current beneficiary forms. Add a durable power of attorney and a healthcare directive, and you have a plan that covers both incapacity and death.
