How to Add a Spouse to a Property Deed

By Ray Dyson, J.D., D.D. August 29, 2026 7 min read
← Back to Blog

Adding a spouse to a property deed is one of the most common real estate documents we prepare. It comes up when someone owned a home before marriage and now wants to put a spouse on title, when spouses want to be on equal legal footing for estate planning purposes, or after a refinance removes a name that needs to be added back.

The process is straightforward when done correctly — but there are important legal considerations to understand before you sign anything.

Why It Matters Which Deed Type You Use

Not all deeds are equal. The deed type you use to add your spouse determines what warranty of title — if any — you are giving them. There are three main types used for this purpose:

Quitclaim Deed

A quitclaim deed transfers whatever interest the grantor (the person conveying) currently has in the property — no more, no less. It makes no warranty that the title is clear or that the grantor actually owns what they're conveying. For adding a spouse to a family home, a quitclaim deed is commonly used and generally appropriate, since you're not selling to a stranger — you're simply sharing your ownership with your partner.

Warranty Deed

A general warranty deed guarantees the title against any defects or claims that have ever existed — going back through the entire chain of title. A special warranty deed only guarantees against defects that arose during the current owner's period of ownership. Warranty deeds are more common in arm's-length sales than in transfers between spouses.

Grant Deed

Used primarily in California and a few other states, a grant deed carries an implied warranty that the grantor hasn't previously conveyed the property to someone else and that the property isn't subject to undisclosed encumbrances made by the grantor.

For most spousal additions, a quitclaim deed is the most practical and commonly used instrument. Your state's requirements determine the exact language, signature requirements, and notarization rules.

How the Process Works

Adding a spouse to a deed involves these steps:

  1. Obtain the current deed. Pull your existing deed from your county recorder's office — either in person or online. You need the legal description of the property, the current vesting (how title is currently held), and the parcel identification number.
  2. Decide how title will be held after the transfer. This is critical (more on this below).
  3. Prepare the new deed. The deed must include the legal description from your current deed, the grantor's name exactly as it appears on the current deed, the grantee names and the new vesting language, and any consideration (usually "$10 and other good and valuable consideration" for a spousal transfer).
  4. Sign in front of a notary. Most states require the grantor to sign before a notary public. Some states also require witnesses.
  5. Record the deed. Take the notarized deed to your county recorder's office (or record electronically if your county allows it) and pay the recording fee. Some states also require a transfer tax return or affidavit of value.

How Should Title Be Held?

Before you prepare the deed, you need to decide how you and your spouse will hold title together. The options have significant legal and estate planning implications:

Joint Tenancy with Right of Survivorship

When one joint tenant dies, their share automatically passes to the surviving joint tenant — outside of probate. This is a popular choice for married couples who want the property to transfer seamlessly at death. All joint tenants must hold equal shares.

Tenancy in Common

Each co-owner holds a specified percentage interest (which can be unequal), and each can pass their share by will or trust to whomever they choose. There is no automatic survivorship right. This is appropriate when you want each spouse to be able to leave their share to someone other than the surviving spouse — for example, children from a prior marriage.

Tenancy by the Entirety

Available only to married couples in states that recognize it (approximately half of U.S. states), tenancy by the entirety provides both survivorship rights and significant asset protection — neither spouse can unilaterally sell, mortgage, or convey their interest without the other's consent, and the property is generally protected from the individual debts of either spouse.

Community Property

In the nine community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin), most property acquired during marriage is community property by default. Some states also allow "community property with right of survivorship" as a title option.

Choosing the wrong vesting can create unintended estate planning consequences. If you have a trust, a prior marriage, or children from outside the current marriage, discuss the vesting decision carefully before recording the deed.

Mortgage Considerations

If there is a mortgage on the property, adding someone to the deed does not add them to the mortgage — and vice versa. The mortgage is a separate obligation. Most mortgages contain a "due-on-sale" clause that technically allows the lender to call the loan due when ownership is transferred. However, the Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982 prohibits lenders from enforcing a due-on-sale clause when a property is transferred to a spouse or child of the borrower, so adding a spouse to the deed should not trigger the mortgage.

That said, if you're adding a spouse with the goal of refinancing to get them on the mortgage as well, you'll handle the mortgage separately through the refinance process.

Tax Considerations

Transfers between spouses are generally not taxable events for federal income tax purposes. However:

  • Some states impose a transfer tax even on spousal transfers — check your state's rules
  • If the property is investment property (not your primary residence), consult a tax professional about basis implications
  • Adding a spouse to the deed of a property with a large unrealized gain could have gift tax implications if you later divorce — less of a concern for most people, but worth knowing

Getting It Done Right

The deed must be drafted using the correct legal description from the current deed, the proper vesting language for your state and your goals, and must comply with your county recorder's formatting requirements. A deed rejected by the recorder's office means you start over and pay again.
