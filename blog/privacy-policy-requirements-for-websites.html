Privacy Policy Requirements for Websites and Online Businesses

September 19, 2026 8 min read
← Back to Blog

If your website collects any information from visitors — an email address, a contact form submission, analytics data, cookies — you almost certainly need a Privacy Policy. What that policy must contain depends on where your users are located, what data you collect, and how you use it. Failure to have a compliant Privacy Policy is not just a reputational risk; it can expose your business to regulatory fines and private lawsuits.

Why Privacy Policies Are Required

Privacy Policies are required by law under multiple overlapping frameworks:

  • The California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) and its amendment, the CPRA, apply to businesses that meet certain thresholds and collect personal information from California residents — regardless of where your business is located
  • The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) applies to any website directed at children under 13, or any site that knowingly collects data from children under 13
  • The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) applies if you collect data from people located in the European Union
  • Many other states now have their own privacy laws: Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Texas, Florida, and others — and more are added each legislative session
  • If you use third-party platforms — Google Analytics, Facebook Pixel, payment processors — those platforms typically require you to have a Privacy Policy as a condition of their terms of service

What Your Privacy Policy Must Cover

Regardless of which laws apply, a complete Privacy Policy should address all of the following:

What Information You Collect

Be specific. Do you collect names, email addresses, phone numbers, payment information, IP addresses, device identifiers, location data, cookies, browsing behavior? List the categories of data you actually collect — not a generic list of everything you might possibly collect someday.

How You Collect It

Do you collect information directly (contact forms, account registrations, purchases) or automatically (cookies, analytics tools, server logs)? Do you receive data from third parties?

How You Use the Information

Common purposes include processing transactions, sending marketing emails, improving the site, complying with legal obligations, and preventing fraud. Users are entitled to know the actual purposes, not vague generalities.

Who You Share It With

Do you share data with payment processors, email marketing platforms, analytics providers, advertising networks, or other third parties? You must disclose this — and if any of those third parties are in other countries, you need to disclose international data transfers.

Data Retention

How long do you keep personal information? This is often overlooked but is required under GDPR and increasingly under U.S. state laws.

User Rights

Users in California, the EU, and many other jurisdictions have the right to know what data you have about them, to request deletion, to opt out of the sale of their data, and to correct inaccurate information. Your Privacy Policy must tell users how to exercise those rights and must include a contact method (email address or form) for doing so.

Cookies and Tracking

If you use cookies — and virtually every website does — you need a cookie disclosure. Under GDPR, you may also need to obtain consent before setting non-essential cookies, which requires a cookie consent banner.

Security

Describe the reasonable security measures you take to protect data. You do not need to reveal your technical architecture, but a general statement about your security practices is expected.

Contact Information

Users must be able to contact you with privacy questions or to exercise their rights. Include an email address or contact form specifically for privacy inquiries.

A Privacy Policy copied from a competitor's website or generated by a generic online tool may be dangerously wrong for your business. The required disclosures depend on what you actually do with user data — a template that doesn't match your real practices is worse than no policy in some respects, because it can be used as evidence of a false representation.

Where to Post Your Privacy Policy

Your Privacy Policy must be easy to find. Best practices include:

  • A link in the footer of every page of your website
  • A link on any form that collects personal information, near the submit button
  • A link in account registration and checkout flows
  • In your email marketing footer (required by the CAN-SPAM Act)
  • In any mobile app through which you collect data

Keeping Your Policy Current

A Privacy Policy is not a one-time document. It must be updated whenever your data practices change — when you add a new analytics tool, start using a new payment processor, begin collecting a new category of data, or when new laws go into effect. Include a "Last Updated" date at the top of your policy and review it at least annually.
