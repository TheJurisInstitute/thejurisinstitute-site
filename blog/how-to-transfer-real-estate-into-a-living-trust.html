How to Transfer Real Estate Into a Living Trust

November 8, 2026 7 min read
← Back to Blog

If you want to know how to transfer real estate into a living trust, you are asking the right question at the right time. Creating a revocable living trust is only half the job. A trust that holds no assets is essentially an empty envelope — it cannot do what you set it up to do. The real work is retitling your property so that ownership passes from your name alone into the name of your trust. When done correctly, that transfer means your home, rental property, or land can bypass probate entirely and pass to your beneficiaries according to your instructions — privately, quickly, and without court involvement.

This article walks through every step of the process in plain language. You do not need to pay an attorney thousands of dollars to accomplish this. With the right document preparation support and a clear understanding of the steps involved, most property owners can complete a trust transfer for a fraction of that cost.

Why Transferring Property Into Your Trust Actually Matters

Many people go through the effort and expense of creating a living trust and then never fund it — meaning they never move assets into it. This is one of the most common and costly estate planning mistakes. If your home is still titled in your name alone when you die, it will likely need to go through probate regardless of what your trust document says. The trust can only control what it owns.

When you successfully transfer real estate into a living trust, you accomplish several things at once. Your home avoids the public probate process. Your successor trustee can step in immediately if you become incapacitated, without needing a court-appointed guardian. Your beneficiaries receive the property on the timeline your trust specifies, not on the court's schedule. And the entire transfer stays out of the public record after your death — unlike probate, which is fully public.

What You Need Before You Start

Before you transfer real estate into a living trust, gather the following:

  • Your executed trust document. The trust must already be signed, dated, and notarized. You cannot transfer property into a trust that does not yet exist in writing.
  • The current deed to the property. You need the exact legal description of the property — not just the street address. The legal description appears on your existing deed and in your county property records.
  • The full legal name of your trust. This is typically formatted as: "[Your Name], Trustee of the [Your Name] Revocable Living Trust dated [Date]." Use this exact phrasing on the new deed.
  • Your mortgage information, if applicable. If your home has a mortgage, you will want to notify your lender before — or immediately after — completing the transfer. More on this below.

Step-by-Step: How to Transfer Real Estate Into a Living Trust

Step 1 — Prepare a New Deed

The vehicle for transferring real estate into a living trust is a new deed. You are not selling the property — you are changing how it is titled. The grantor (the person conveying the property) is you as an individual. The grantee (who receives it) is you in your capacity as trustee of your trust.

The type of deed you use depends on your state and your situation. A quitclaim deed conveys whatever interest you currently hold, without any warranties. It is commonly used for trust transfers because you are essentially transferring to yourself. A grant deed or warranty deed makes certain guarantees about the title and is more common in some states, particularly for real estate transactions between unrelated parties. For a trust funding transfer, a quitclaim deed is usually appropriate and widely accepted — but check your state's norms or work with a document preparation specialist who knows your jurisdiction.

The deed must include the full legal description of the property exactly as it appears in the public record. A street address alone is not sufficient and will likely result in rejection when you try to record it.

Step 2 — Name the Trust Correctly as Grantee

This step trips up more people than any other. A trust is not a legal entity the way a corporation is — it cannot technically "own" property by itself. What you are actually doing is conveying property to yourself as trustee. The correct format is:

[Your Full Name], as Trustee of the [Your Full Name] Revocable Living Trust, dated [Month Day, Year]

Some states allow a shortened version. Others require the full trustee designation. If the grantee line is not worded precisely, the county recorder may reject the deed, or worse, accept it in a form that creates title ambiguity later. Take the time to get this right.

Step 3 — Sign and Notarize the Deed

You must sign the deed in front of a notary public. The notary will verify your identity and affix their seal and signature. Some states also require one or two witnesses in addition to the notary — check your state's specific requirements. Without proper notarization, the county will not record the deed, and the transfer will not be legally effective.

Important: Do not sign the deed before you are in front of the notary. A pre-signed deed is not validly notarized, and many notaries will refuse to notarize it after the fact. Bring the unsigned deed, your government-issued photo ID, and your original trust document to the notary appointment.

Step 4 — Record the Deed With Your County

Recording is what makes the transfer legally effective and public. Take the original notarized deed to your county recorder's office (sometimes called the register of deeds, land records office, or clerk of court, depending on your state). You will pay a recording fee — typically between $15 and $50 for the first page, with additional fees for each subsequent page. Some counties allow recording by mail; others require in-person submission.

Once recorded, you will receive a stamped copy of the deed showing the document number, recording date, and book and page reference in the public record. Keep this in a safe place with your original trust document. At this point, the transfer of real estate into your living trust is complete.

Step 5 — Update Your Homeowner's Insurance

Contact your homeowner's insurance carrier after recording the deed and let them know the property is now held in trust. Ask them to add your trust as an additional named insured on the policy. Most insurers handle this routinely and at no additional cost. Failure to notify your insurer does not necessarily invalidate your coverage, but it can complicate a future claim. This is a quick phone call or online form submission — do not skip it.

Step 6 — Notify Your Mortgage Lender

If you have a mortgage on the property, your loan documents almost certainly contain a due-on-sale clause, which technically allows your lender to call the full loan balance due if ownership of the property changes. Federal law, however, provides an important exception: the Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982 prohibits lenders from triggering the due-on-sale clause when a borrower transfers property to an inter vivos (living) trust in which the borrower remains a beneficiary and continues to occupy the home as a primary residence.

In plain terms: a trust transfer to your own revocable living trust does not give your lender the right to accelerate your mortgage, as long as you remain the beneficiary and primary occupant. That said, it is still a best practice to notify your lender in writing and keep a copy of that notification with your records. Some lenders may require documentation of the trust or ask you to complete a simple acknowledgment form.

Note on rental or investment property: The Garn-St. Germain protection applies specifically to your primary residence. If you are transferring a rental property or vacation home into a living trust, review your loan documents carefully and consider reaching out to your lender before recording the deed. The rules can differ, and you want to avoid an inadvertent default.

What Happens to Property Taxes After the Transfer?

Transferring real estate into a revocable living trust does not trigger a property tax reassessment in most states. Because you remain the beneficial owner of the trust and retain full control during your lifetime, taxing authorities generally treat this as a non-event for property tax purposes. California, for instance, has explicit rules under Proposition 19 addressing trust transfers. Florida, Texas, and most other states similarly do not reassess based on a transfer to your own revocable trust.

That said, property tax rules are set at the state and county level and can vary. If you are in a state with unusual rules — or if your property is a commercial holding — verify your local rules before recording the deed. A document preparation specialist familiar with your state can help you confirm the correct approach.

Community Property States: One Additional Consideration

If you are married and live in a community property state — Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, or Wisconsin — real estate acquired during the marriage is typically owned jointly as community property. When you transfer real estate into a living trust in a community property state, both spouses must generally sign the deed, and both should ideally be named as co-trustees of a joint trust or as individual trustees of their respective trusts. Getting this right matters because community property carries significant tax advantages at death that can be lost if the property is improperly titled.

Can You Do This Without a Lawyer?

Yes — and many property owners successfully complete a trust transfer on their own or with the help of a document preparation service. The key is using the correct deed form for your state, drafting the grantee designation precisely, and completing the recording process correctly. Where people run into problems is when they use a generic deed template that does not comply with their state's specific formatting requirements, or when they improperly identify the trustee on the grantee line.

A Transfer Worth Making

A living trust that never gets funded is a wasted opportunity. If your goal is to keep your home out of probate, protect your family's privacy, and give your successor trustee the ability to act quickly, the transfer of your real estate into the trust is the step that makes those goals real.
