LLC vs. Corporation: Which Structure Is Right for Your Business?

October 10, 2026 9 min read
← Back to Blog

When you decide to formally organize your business, the two most common choices are a Limited Liability Company (LLC) and a corporation. Both provide the liability protection that separates your personal assets from your business obligations. Beyond that, they operate very differently — in how they are taxed, how they are governed, and what they signal to investors and partners.

The right choice depends on your specific situation. Here is how they compare on every dimension that matters.

The Fundamental Difference: Governance and Flexibility

An LLC is a flexible, relatively informal entity. It is governed by its Operating Agreement, which the members (owners) write themselves. You can structure an LLC however you want — different classes of ownership, custom profit-sharing arrangements, unique management structures — as long as it complies with your state's LLC statute. An LLC can have one owner or many, and there is no requirement for formal meetings, minutes, or resolutions.

A corporation is a more structured, formal entity governed by its bylaws, a board of directors, and state corporation statutes. Corporations have shareholders who own stock, a board of directors that oversees the company, and officers who manage day-to-day operations. Corporations are required to hold annual meetings, maintain minutes, pass formal resolutions for major decisions, and follow their state's corporate formalities. Failure to follow corporate formalities can expose shareholders to personal liability — a risk called "piercing the corporate veil."

Taxation: The Most Important Difference for Most Businesses

For most small business owners, the tax treatment is the deciding factor.

LLC taxation: By default, a single-member LLC is treated as a "disregarded entity" for tax purposes (reported on the owner's personal tax return on Schedule C). A multi-member LLC is treated as a partnership. In both cases, income "passes through" to the owners, who pay income tax at their individual rates. LLCs can also elect to be taxed as an S-Corporation or C-Corporation, giving them access to corporate tax treatment if that is more advantageous.

C-Corporation taxation: A standard corporation (C-Corp) is taxed as a separate entity. It pays corporate income tax on its profits. When the corporation distributes profits to shareholders as dividends, those dividends are taxed again on the shareholders' personal returns. This "double taxation" is the most common criticism of the C-Corp structure. However, C-Corps also have access to certain deductions and benefits not available to pass-through entities, and the current 21% flat corporate tax rate may be advantageous in some situations.

S-Corporation taxation: An S-Corp is a corporation that has elected pass-through taxation. Income passes through to shareholders and is reported on their personal returns, avoiding double taxation. However, S-Corps have significant restrictions: no more than 100 shareholders, all shareholders must be U.S. citizens or residents, only one class of stock is allowed, and certain entities cannot be S-Corp shareholders. An LLC with an S-Corp election can achieve similar tax treatment with more structural flexibility.

Many small business owners form LLCs and elect S-Corporation tax treatment to get the best of both worlds: the operational flexibility of an LLC with the payroll tax savings the S-Corp election can provide. This is a strategy worth discussing with a tax professional before you form your entity.

Liability Protection: Similar, With Important Differences

Both LLCs and corporations protect the owners' personal assets from business liabilities. If your business is sued or cannot pay its debts, creditors generally cannot come after your personal bank accounts, home, or other assets — as long as you maintain the separation between your personal and business finances.

However, this protection is not absolute in either structure. Courts can pierce the corporate or LLC veil and hold owners personally liable when:

  • Owners commingle personal and business funds
  • The entity is used to commit fraud
  • Corporate formalities are completely ignored (more relevant to corporations)
  • The entity is undercapitalized relative to its obligations

Investors and Capital Raising

If you plan to raise venture capital or bring in institutional investors, a Delaware C-Corporation is effectively required. The VC industry has standardized on the C-Corp structure: it allows preferred stock (the preferred return structure investors require), unlimited shareholders, and easy stock option plans for employees. LLCs can issue equity, but the pass-through taxation creates complications for institutional investors (particularly tax-exempt investors like pension funds), and the lack of standardized equity instruments creates friction in deals.

For a business that is not on a venture capital trajectory — the vast majority of small businesses — the C-Corp's investor-friendly features are irrelevant, and the LLC's simplicity and flexibility are advantages.

A Quick Decision Framework

Choose an LLC if you are:

  • A solo business owner or a small group of partners in an operating business
  • Not planning to raise institutional equity financing
  • Seeking simplicity and flexibility in governance
  • Considering an S-Corp tax election to manage self-employment taxes

Choose a corporation (specifically a Delaware C-Corp) if you are:

  • Planning to raise venture capital or angel investment
  • Planning to issue stock options to employees through a formal equity plan
  • Planning an eventual IPO or acquisition by a public company
  • Operating in an industry where the C-Corp structure is expected by counterparties
