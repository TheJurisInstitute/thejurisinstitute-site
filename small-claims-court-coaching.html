Small Claims Court Education

Small Claims Court: The People's Court

Small claims court is the most accessible part of the American legal system. It is designed to allow ordinary people to resolve relatively small disputes — typically ranging from $2,500 to $25,000 depending on the state — without hiring an attorney. The procedures are simpler, the rules of evidence are relaxed, and the process moves faster than regular civil court. In most states, the entire process from filing to hearing takes 30 to 90 days.

The reality of small claims court is this: approximately 90% of the people in the courtroom on any given day are not represented by a lawyer. They are business owners, landlords, tenants, contractors, consumers, neighbors, and individuals with a dispute they want resolved. Most of them have never been to court before. Most of them do not know the specific procedures for their state or county. And most of them are making avoidable mistakes that cost them their case.

The mistakes most people make in small claims court are not complicated ones. They bring the wrong evidence. They fail to organize what they bring. They do not understand what the judge needs to hear. They present their case emotionally rather than factually. They fail to respond to the defendant's arguments. They do not know how to object to improper evidence from the other side. These are mistakes that coaching eliminates.

What Judges Look for in Small Claims

Small claims judges are experienced. They have seen hundreds or thousands of cases. They know within the first few minutes of your presentation whether you have organized your case or whether you are going to waste their time. They are looking for: a clear, concise statement of what happened and what you are owed; documentation that supports your claim — contracts, receipts, photos, text messages, estimates; a specific dollar amount with a clear calculation; and a presentation that stays on the facts without emotional drama. We teach you how to present exactly what judges need to see.

Defendants Have Rights Too

If you have been sued in small claims court, you are not powerless. As a defendant, you can: contest the plaintiff's claims and require them to prove every element; file a counterclaim if you have your own claim against the plaintiff; challenge improper service of process; raise affirmative defenses including payment, release, and statute of limitations; and negotiate a settlement before the hearing. Our defendant coaching covers all of these options and prepares you to present your defense effectively.

Who We Serve

Who Needs Small Claims Court Coaching?

Anyone filing or defending a small claims case benefits from professional coaching. The edge goes to the prepared party — and most people in small claims court are anything but prepared.

Individuals Owed Money

If someone owes you money — a former friend, a contractor who did not finish the job, a business that refused your refund, a tenant who owes back rent — small claims court is often the most efficient path to judgment. We help you file correctly and win.

Landlords Seeking Unpaid Rent or Damages

Landlords use small claims court regularly to recover unpaid rent, security deposit disputes, and property damage beyond the deposit. Proper documentation and presentation of landlord-tenant claims is essential — we know exactly what judges look for.

Tenants Seeking Deposit Returns

Tenants are often owed security deposits that landlords refuse to return without justification. Small claims court is the proper venue to recover an improperly withheld deposit, and tenants may be entitled to additional damages under their state's landlord-tenant statute.

Consumers with Contractor Disputes

Contractor disputes — work not performed, poor workmanship, refusal to refund a deposit — are among the most common small claims cases. We help consumers document the breach, calculate damages, and present the case in the organized format that wins.

Businesses Collecting Small Debts

Small businesses regularly face non-paying customers whose balances are too small to justify full litigation but too significant to write off. Small claims court is an efficient collection tool, and repeat filers benefit from a proven filing and presentation system.

Defendants Who Have Been Sued

If you have been served with a small claims complaint, you need to respond — either by appearing on the hearing date with your evidence, by filing a counterclaim, or by negotiating a settlement. Ignoring a small claims complaint results in a default judgment against you.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can I sue for in small claims court?

Small claims court dollar limits vary by state and sometimes by county or court type. Most states allow claims between $5,000 and $10,000, but some go higher — California allows up to $12,500 for individuals ($6,250 for businesses), and some states allow claims up to $25,000. If your claim exceeds the small claims limit, you can either reduce it to fit within the limit (waiving the excess) or file in regular civil court. We advise you on your state's current limit and whether small claims is the right venue for your specific claim amount.

Do I need a lawyer for small claims court?

In most states, attorneys are either not permitted to appear in small claims court at all, or their appearance is discouraged. The system is specifically designed for people to represent themselves. This levels the playing field — both parties typically appear without counsel. What gives one party an advantage over the other is preparation: organized evidence, a clear presentation, and an understanding of what the judge needs to hear. That is exactly what our coaching provides.

What evidence should I bring to small claims court?

Bring every piece of documentation that supports your claim — original contracts or agreements, receipts, invoices, estimates, bank statements, cancelled checks, photographs (printed, not just on your phone), text message screenshots (printed), emails (printed), and any written communications with the other party. Organize everything chronologically or by topic, bring multiple copies (one for the judge, one for yourself, one for the other party), and prepare a simple summary that walks the judge through what happened and what you are owed. We help you identify exactly what to bring and how to organize it.

What happens if I win but the other party refuses to pay?

A small claims judgment does not enforce itself. If the defendant does not pay voluntarily, you must take additional steps to collect. These include: wage garnishment (ordering the defendant's employer to withhold wages), bank levy (freezing and seizing funds from the defendant's bank account), recording an abstract of judgment (creating a lien on the defendant's real property), and conducting a debtor's examination (requiring the defendant to appear in court and disclose their assets under oath). We provide coaching on all of these collection tools and prepare the underlying documents.

Can I appeal a small claims judgment?

Yes. Either party can appeal a small claims judgment to the regular civil court in most states, where the case is typically heard de novo (tried fresh, not just reviewed for error). Appeals must be filed within a short deadline — typically 30 days from the entry of judgment, though the deadline varies by state. An appeal escalates the case to a more formal proceeding where the rules of evidence apply more strictly and, in many states, attorneys are permitted. We provide coaching on the appeal process and can prepare the notice of appeal and supporting documents if you choose to appeal.
