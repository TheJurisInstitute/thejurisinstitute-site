The Pro Se Legal
Survival Checklist

12 critical steps to take before you represent yourself in any legal matter — whether you are filing your own court documents, responding to a lawsuit, or navigating a legal process without an attorney.

The Juris Institute — 25+ Years of Legal Experience

Representing yourself in a legal matter — known as appearing "pro se" — is your constitutional right. But the legal system was built by lawyers, for lawyers. Missing a deadline, using the wrong form, or failing to follow local court rules can cost you your case before it ever begins. This checklist is designed to help you avoid the most common and most costly mistakes.

Before You File Anything

01
Identify the correct court and jurisdiction
Every legal matter belongs in a specific court — federal vs. state, civil vs. criminal, trial court vs. small claims. Filing in the wrong court means your case gets dismissed and you lose filing fees. Look up your state's court system website and verify that you are filing in the court with proper subject matter jurisdiction and geographic jurisdiction over your matter.
Most common pro se mistake
02
Look up and download the correct forms
Most courts have official forms available on their website. Using outdated, unofficial, or incorrect forms is grounds for rejection. Always download forms directly from the court's official website — never from a third-party site that may be outdated. If the court does not provide a form, you will need to draft the document yourself or have it professionally prepared.
03
Read the local court rules — all of them
Every court has local rules that supplement the state or federal rules of procedure. These cover margin sizes, font requirements, page limits, filing deadlines, service requirements, and more. Courts routinely reject documents that fail to follow local rules, even when the substance is correct. Local rules are almost always available on the court's website under a "Local Rules" or "Court Rules" section.
Do not skip this step
04
Know your statute of limitations
Every legal claim has a deadline — called a statute of limitations — after which you lose your right to bring that claim forever, regardless of how strong your case is. These deadlines vary by type of claim and by state, ranging from one year to six years or more. If you are the party responding to a lawsuit, you typically have 20-30 days to file an Answer after being served. Missing either type of deadline is almost always fatal to your case.
Deadlines are absolute

Preparing Your Documents

05
Organize all facts and evidence before you write
Legal documents are formal written instruments, not stories. Before drafting anything, write out a clear chronological timeline of events, gather all relevant contracts, correspondence, receipts, and records, and identify every party involved. Courts evaluate documents based on specific factual allegations tied to specific legal elements. Vague or disorganized facts weaken even strong claims.
06
Use precise, formal language — not casual language
Legal documents must be written in formal language, using specific legal terms correctly. "Plaintiff" and "Defendant" — not "I" and "them." "Respondent" in family law matters, "Petitioner" in appeals. Courts take informal or emotional language as a sign that the party does not understand the proceedings. Keep legal documents factual, formal, and structured around numbered paragraphs.
07
Meet the formatting requirements exactly
Most courts require: 8.5" x 11" paper, 1-inch margins on all sides, 12-point Times New Roman or similar serif font for text, double-spaced body paragraphs, and line numbers in the left margin (required in many California and federal courts). Some courts have specific cover page requirements, caption formats, and certificate of service requirements. When in doubt, look at a sample filing from the same court.
08
Include every required element for your document type
A Complaint must allege facts supporting each element of each legal claim. An Answer must admit or deny each paragraph. A Motion must include a statement of facts, the applicable legal standard, argument, and a clear statement of the relief requested. Appellate briefs require a table of contents, table of authorities, statement of jurisdiction, statement of the case, issues presented, standard of review, argument, and conclusion. Omitting required elements is grounds for dismissal or denial.
Know your document type's required sections

Filing and Service

09
Understand service of process rules
After filing a Complaint, you must formally "serve" the defendant — deliver a copy of the lawsuit in a manner the law recognizes. In most states, service must be done by a disinterested third party (not you), often a sheriff, process server, or adult who is not a party. Improper service means the court has no jurisdiction over the defendant, and your case can be dismissed. Once you respond to a lawsuit yourself, service between the parties typically becomes simpler — by mail or electronic service.
10
Keep copies of everything you file
Every document you submit to the court should be copied — one copy for the court file (conformed with a file-stamp), one copy for your own records, and one copy for each opposing party. Courts can lose documents. Opposing parties will dispute whether they received service. Your copy is your proof. Keep everything in a dedicated folder, organized by date, and note the date, time, and method of every filing and service.
11
File a Certificate of Service with every document
Every document filed with the court after the initial Complaint must include a Certificate of Service (sometimes called Proof of Service) stating how, when, and to whom a copy was sent to the opposing party. Without this, the court may reject your filing or refuse to rule on your motion. The format is simple — one short paragraph at the end of your filing or on a separate page — but it is required on virtually every subsequent filing.
12
Never miss a court-ordered deadline
Once a court sets a deadline — for discovery responses, motions, briefing, or hearings — missing it can result in a default judgment against you, dismissal of your case, or sanctions. Put every deadline in writing immediately, set calendar reminders 7 days and 2 days in advance, and if you cannot meet a deadline, file a Motion for Extension of Time before the deadline passes. Courts rarely grant late requests after the fact.
Calendaring is life or death in litigation

Bonus: Pro Se Best Practices

A
Visit your court's self-help center or law library
Most courthouse have a self-help center or legal aid clinic where court staff (not attorneys) can answer procedural questions. County law libraries also provide access to legal research databases and form books. These are free resources — use them.
B
Research every case you cite — and know what it says
If you cite case law in a motion or brief, read the full case — not just a summary. Courts have sanctioned pro se parties for citing cases that say the opposite of what was claimed. Free legal research is available at Google Scholar (scholar.google.com) and CourtListener (courtlistener.com).
C
Stay professional and unemotional in every filing
Judges read thousands of documents. Emotional language, personal attacks on opposing counsel, and hyperbole all undermine your credibility immediately. State facts. Cite authority. Request specific relief. Courts respect substance over passion.
